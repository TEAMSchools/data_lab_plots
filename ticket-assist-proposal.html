Where this actually helps

Most of our ticket volume isn't novel — it's repeat traffic.

Since 2023 we've logged roughly 36,000 tickets across regional and central queues. A large share of that volume is Tier 1 by nature: the same handful of question types, asked by different people, month after month. That's the traffic this tool is built for — not the hard cases.

Good candidates for AI-drafted replies

  • Standard data/report requests with a known format precedent
  • Policy and process questions answered in a Help Center article
  • Access and account requests with a documented procedure
  • "How do I..." questions with a clear, current article match

Stays with a human, always

  • Anything involving a specific student or family record
  • Personnel, compensation, or HR case matters
  • Requests with no confident match — routed to escalation, not guessed at
  • Anything from outside our own staff domains

These groupings are a starting hypothesis, not a claim of measured impact — validating which ticket types actually get confident, correct matches is the job of the evaluation phase, not something we assume up front.

How this stays safe by default

Four guardrails, non-negotiable in phase one

01

Internal only

Only fires for requesters on our own staff domains (kippteamandfamily.org, kippmiami.org, and other regional domains). Everyone else flows through the normal queue, untouched.

02

Always disclosed

Every AI reply is visibly labeled. No one mistakes it for a person, and every reply carries an easy way to reach one.

03

No confident match, no guess

Below a measured confidence threshold, the tool doesn't respond at all — it escalates with a plain "we couldn't find an immediate answer" note.

04

Humans hold the close button

The AI never marks a ticket solved. Only the requester (via reply or link) or a staff member can close it, until we've proven the tool out.

Technical overview

How a ticket actually moves through the system

Everything here is one workflow, triggered by ordinary Zendesk events. Nothing runs unless a ticket comes in on an internal domain.

End-to-end flow

1

Ticket created in Zendesk

Requester emails a queue as usual (e.g. data, IT, HR). No change to how tickets are created.

Zendesk Support
2

Webhook fires the orchestrator

A "ticket created" trigger calls a small Python service, hosted as a Dagster job with a webhook-triggered sensor.

Zendesk webhookPythonDagster sensor
3

Domain gate

Requester email checked against the internal-domain allowlist before anything else runs. Non-matches exit here — the ticket proceeds exactly as it would today.

Config table
4

Orchestrator hands off to an agent

Rather than one fixed step, the orchestrator hands the ticket to a Claude agent that picks from a few tools depending on what it actually needs. To look for a written answer, it searches our Help Center articles and past resolved tickets through Zendesk's own MCP server — starting with the same queue the ticket came from, since that's usually where the closest precedent lives. If the question is really about a live number instead of a written answer (an attendance export, an enrollment count), it queries our Cube semantic layer directly instead of digging up a past answer that may already be out of date.

Zendesk MCPCube semantic layer
5

Confidence gate

Before drafting anything, the agent has to say how confident it is in what it found. Confident enough, and it moves on to writing a reply. Not confident — or nothing relevant turned up at all — and the ticket skips straight to being routed to a person instead.

Confidence rubric (set during evaluation)
6

Response drafted

Claude generates a reply grounded only in the matched article or prior resolution — with mandatory AI-disclosure language, an escalation offer, and close-out instructions baked into the prompt.

Claude APIZendesk MCP: get_article / get_ticket
7

Posted back to the ticket

Reply posted as a public comment. Status set to Pending (never Solved), tagged ai-responded, queue tag preserved. If the agent couldn't fully answer it, it also looks at who resolved similar tickets before and assigns this one straight to that staff member, instead of leaving it for someone to notice in a shared queue.

Zendesk MCP: post_comment / tag_ticket / assign_ticket
8

Two ways it resolves

Requester clicks "✓ This solved my issue" or replies to say it's handled → a small webhook marks the ticket Solved. Otherwise, it's already sitting with the staff member the agent assigned it to, tagged ai-responded so it's easy to spot during review. Either way, the outcome gets logged, so over time we can see what's actually working and adjust the tool accordingly.

What each side sees in Zendesk

requester

  • Ticket created and confirmed exactly as today — no visible change up front.
  • A reply arrives labeled clearly as AI-generated, not from a person.
  • Reply includes the answer, a link to close the ticket if it's resolved, and a plain way to ask for a person instead.
  • Ticket stays in Pending — it never looks silently closed.
  • If no confident answer exists, they get a short note that it's being escalated — not a stall, not a guess.

staff / agents

  • Each ticket the AI can't fully close is assigned directly to whoever's handled similar requests before, based on ticket history — not just dropped into a shared queue for someone to notice.
  • A saved view still filters to the ai-responded tag, so there's always a full list to check, even before anything's been picked up.
  • Agents can edit, replace, or approve the reply, or leave it if the requester already confirmed it worked.
  • The AI never sets a ticket to Solved — that stays a person's or the requester's call in this phase.
  • Escalated (low-confidence) tickets get the same smart assignment, tagged needs-human.

Evaluation

How we'll know it's actually good

Two separate things need measuring: whether retrieval finds the right source, and whether the generated reply is grounded, correctly toned, and escalates when it should. Promptfoo runs both as a repeatable suite, not a one-time gut check.

Building the test set

Zendesk doesn't record "which article solved this ticket," so we build a labeled set by hand from historical solved tickets: take a past ticket's opening request as the query, and hand-label the article or prior ticket that should count as the correct match. A few hundred labeled pairs, sampled across queues, is enough to start.

Sampling should mirror real queue volume — if data tickets are 40% of traffic, they should be roughly 40% of the test set, not an afterthought.

What we measure

MetricWhat it catchesHow
retrieval precisionWrong or irrelevant matches ranked highlyCompare top-k retrieved results against the labeled correct match
retrieval recallCorrect match exists but never surfacesSame labeled set, checking if the right source appears in top-k at all
groundednessReply drifting beyond the matched source (hallucination)LLM-graded rubric: does every claim in the reply trace back to the matched article/ticket
escalation correctnessConfidently answering things it shouldn'tTest cases with no good match in the corpus — reply must escalate, not guess
false-escalate rateEscalating things it could have answered — the failure that quietly kills adoptionTest cases with a clear correct match — reply must not skip to escalation
tone & disclosureMissing AI-disclosure language, wrong registerLLM-graded rubric checking required phrases and tone are present

Setting the confidence bar

We no longer compute our own similarity score — Zendesk's MCP server handles the actual search, and the agent reports back how confident it is in what it found. So instead of plotting a similarity cutoff, we run the agent against the labeled set and check, ticket by ticket, whether "confident" actually meant "correct." The bar sits wherever confident-and-right cleanly separates from confident-and-wrong — and we set it to favor precision over recall at launch: a missed match just escalates like normal (no cost), while a wrong confident answer is the expensive failure.

# conceptual promptfoo test case
- description: "data queue — known report-format match"
  vars:
    ticket_text: "Need Q3 attendance export by school"
    expected_source: "ticket_41007"
  assert:
    - type: contains-source-id
    - type: llm-rubric
      value: "reply only references content from the matched source, includes AI-disclosure language, offers escalation"

When the suite runs

  • Before shipping any prompt or confidence-bar change — a hard gate, not a suggestion
  • On a recurring weekly schedule, since ticket volume keeps growing and how well "confident" tracks "correct" can drift as it does

The feedback loop we get for free

"This answered it — close ticket" and "Escalate to a person" aren't just UI conveniences — they're a label, generated at zero extra cost, on every single AI-answered ticket. Where the hand-labeled test set gives us a few hundred judgments made once, this gives us a real-time judgment on every ticket, made by the person who actually asked the question.

→

Requester clicks "this answered it"

Logged as an implicit positive: that (ticket text → matched source → generated reply) triple gets folded into the labeled set as a new confirmed-correct example.

→

Requester clicks "escalate" or replies instead

Logged as an implicit negative. Worth distinguishing two failure modes here: the match was wrong (retrieval problem) versus the match was right but the reply was unclear or incomplete (generation problem) — the human agent's eventual resolution tells us which.

→

Silence — no click either way

Not neutral. Worth treating as its own category (unclear reply? requester disengaged? ticket forgotten?) rather than discarding, since a growing "no signal" rate is itself a warning sign about reply quality or clarity.

This turns the hand-labeled Promptfoo set from a one-time scaffold into a growing, continuously-refreshed dataset — the confidence threshold and the prompt itself can both be re-tuned periodically against real outcomes instead of staying frozen at launch settings.

A/B testing, given the volume we have

At tens of thousands of tickets a year across several queues, we have enough traffic to treat this as a genuine experimentation problem rather than a ship-and-hope rollout. A few things worth testing this way rather than deciding by intuition:

  • Confidence threshold variants — run two thresholds concurrently on random ticket splits within a queue, compare close-rate vs. escalate-rate vs. eventual human correction rate
  • Prompt / tone variants — does a more concise reply out-perform a more explanatory one on close-vs-escalate rate, controlling for the same matched source
  • Query construction — raw ticket text vs. a cleaned-up query (stripping pleasantries, keeping the actual ask) handed to Zendesk's search, held out by ticket cohort, compared against the same outcome labels
  • Holdout for causal impact — a small, randomly withheld slice of otherwise-eligible tickets gets normal human-only handling, so we can honestly attribute changes in resolution time or reopen rate to the tool itself, not just to seasonal ticket-mix shifts
Worth sizing this properly before running it — a queue's weekly ticket volume determines how long a test needs to run to detect a real difference in close-rate, not just how long feels reasonable. A quick power calculation up front avoids reading noise as a result.

This is genuinely a data science workstream in its own right, not a one-off setting to tune once — assignment logic, guardrails against contaminating cohorts (e.g. the same requester landing in different arms across tickets), and a standing review cadence for results all need real ownership.

Implementation guide

Step by step, start to pilot

Eight stages, roughly in build order. Later stages assume earlier ones are working — this isn't meant to be parallelized on a first pass.

01

Zendesk API access

Create a dedicated service account (not a personal login) and generate an API token scoped to it. Confirm scopes cover ticket read/write, comment creation, tagging, and Help Center read.

  • API token + subdomain stored as secrets, not in code
  • Webhook subscriptions created: ticket created, ticket status changed to solved
  • Confirm rate limits for our plan tier before assuming call volume is free
02

Domain allowlist & queue taxonomy

Define the internal-domain list (kippteamandfamily.org, kippmiami.org, and other regional domains) and the queue/group mapping (data, IT, HR, etc.) as a small config table — not hardcoded — since domains and queues will grow as regions are added.

03

Connect the agent to Zendesk MCP and Cube

No server for us to build here — confirm Zendesk's MCP server covers what the agent needs (search articles and past tickets, read a full article or ticket, post a comment, tag, and assign), and give the agent access alongside our existing Cube MCP connection for live data queries.

  • Confirm Zendesk's MCP server can assign a ticket to a specific person; if it can't yet, that's a small gap to close before pilot
  • Reuse our existing Cube MCP connection for live data queries — no new service to stand up
  • Scope every write action (comment, tag, assign) to internal-only tickets, same as any other write tool
04

Write the prompts

System prompt enforces: answer only from the matched source, always include AI-disclosure language, always offer escalation and the close-out phrase/link, never fabricate beyond the retrieved content. Include a handful of real (anonymized) past tickets as few-shot examples of the tone we want.

05

The orchestrator script

The service that ties it together on each webhook event: domain check → agent searches via Zendesk MCP (or queries Cube directly for a numbers-shaped question) → confidence gate → generate reply, or route to a person and assign it based on who's handled similar tickets before → post the reply and tag the ticket → set status to Pending. A second, much smaller endpoint handles the close-out click/reply, independent of everything else.

06

Orchestration & scheduling in Dagster

Down to one job instead of four: a webhook-triggered sensor that fires the orchestrator on every new ticket. There's no separate indexing schedule to maintain, since search runs against Zendesk's own live data instead of a copy we'd have to keep in sync.

07

Wire up the Promptfoo suite

Build the labeled test set from historical tickets, score retrieval precision/recall and generation groundedness/escalation, and use those results to set the confidence bar — not before this step, since guessing a bar first just means redoing it.

08

Pilot rollout

Start with a single queue (the one with the most repetitive, well-documented traffic — likely data or IT). Every AI-answered ticket gets reviewed by a person during the pilot window. Only after we have real accuracy numbers from that review do we talk about loosening to spot-checks or adding a second queue.
